## 2. The verdicts

**The blind reader** read only the book: no design document, card or note.
- **4 / 5 as a reader and 3.5 / 5 as an acquiring editor.** Would read book two.
- The voice holds for the whole length, and the strange stays strange.
- **As an editor, would buy it only with a cut:**
  - it repeats its engine;
  - the opposition is faceless, so the climax has no one to push against;
  - the finale reads as a data point.
- **Asked to say what the book is about,** it named its central question accurately: what you owe people when you cannot
  explain what is happening to them, and whose account it is to give.

**The character readers** found every principal holds together, and no guard is breached anywhere:
- **Seraphine** is the most fully realised and acts more than she reacts. Her expertise moves the plot.
- **Lucien** is one of the best-built. His point of view notices structure first. His English drifts from formal to
  American idiom.
- **Baz** is distinct, and his method is credible. He never sees a box or the collector, and nothing foreshadows his death.
- **Caro**'s voice and medic's register are her own. Her privacy line leaks once (E42).
- **Inez** is among the strongest secondary characters, but has no card.
- **Trip** is consistent, and her pronouns are correct throughout. The page's working bartender differs from the cards'
  glamour.
- **The presences** read as two distinct entities. **Elisabet** is the strongest cameo.

**Where the page and a card disagree, the page usually reads better.** Seraphine's early, uninvited arrivals; Lucien
escalating where his card calls him a brake. **Those are recorded as card updates, not page faults.**

**The narration readers** found Calder's voice in all three acts, with Act III the closest to it:
- The old contrast family is largely fixed.
- **A new layer of stock phrases has replaced it.** The worst is a characterising simile comparing someone to a person
  who has had some experience, used by every point of view about 45 times.
- **The explaining has moved to just after the peaks,** usually as an epigram.
- **A few point-of-view slips** fall at load-bearing moments.

**Vocabulary.** The problem is overloaded words more than worn ones:
- the word for *line* carries about ten senses;
- the forecast has five names;
- *window* names both the forecast and the glass the events break;
- record nouns thicken by act, as times do.

**Counting against action.** The author's 09-29 concern is half answered:
- **62% of 130 counting scenes now lead to an action, a decision or a cost.** Only 12% put a protagonist at risk.
- **Act II mostly counts to decide how to count.**
- **11 of the 14 events on the page end as a written line.**
- **E48 is where counting stands in for action:** its two risky acts are reported, not shown.

**Seraphine's agency** came through the three passes intact:
- her share is 40.7% of the book, about the same as before the passes;
- every ruled obligation is still on the page, in order;
- one fact needs restoring: a rhythm-pass cut in E46 blurred which Sunday reading she means.

## 3. The consolidation

Every high- and medium-severity finding and every factual claim was checked against the text.
- **7 findings were rejected as false.** The most important: a claim that a character's race is marked at E06 (no one's
  is). Two of Claude's own spot-checks also turned out to be non-defects: Miss Tavie's surname at E49, and the two
  *seventeenths*.
- **What remains:**
  - 50 corrections, and 7 source-side corrections;
  - 20 line and narration patterns;
  - 14 structural cuts, including the times block;
  - **31 author decisions.**

**Convergence.** The most-raised findings, each named by four or more readers:
- the stock-phrase family;
- explaining after peaks;
- epigram and double endings;
- the senders' hints;
- E48's recaps and its reported actions;
- tags shared between characters, such as the pencil behind the ear.
