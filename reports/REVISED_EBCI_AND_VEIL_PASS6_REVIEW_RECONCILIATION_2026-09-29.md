## 4. The trilogy conflict trajectories

The review supports the author's idea of trilogy-specific expectations: **each trilogy opens with people struggling
to work together, and increasingly tests what they can do together against outside opposition.** It adds these
distinctions:
- **Less internal obstruction, not less internal difference.** By a trilogy's end they disagree hard and still act
  together.
- **Three things are tracked separately:**
  - personal pressure;
  - conflict among protagonists;
  - external opposition (the phenomenon's danger does not count as an antagonist).
- **At the climax,** internal conflict falls in frequency and rises in importance. External conflict grows in reach and
  consequence, not in frequency.
- **Floors for dramatic function, ceilings for repetition.** These are review triggers, not quotas. **No numbers yet.**
- **Across the saga:**
  - the group gets better at cooperating, so later disagreements concern substantive choices;
  - the opposition becomes entangled with their own aims (Veil: what is happening; Neon: attractive, costly promises;
    Loom: what world their intervention makes).
- **For now:**
  - B01 needs enough external action to drive its mystery;
  - **B03 must not be saturated with fresh fractures.** Readers need to experience what this group has built before
    they lose Baz.

**Claude's view:** this is sound, and it fits the approved order. The review proposes a compact saga-level guide, then
an act-level review of the existing beats, and no numerical floors until the distribution is seen. That is small and
analytical, not another design round. **The B03 point bears directly on pass 6,** which adds thirteen B03 revisions:
F5's recasting should favour **cooperation under strain** in B03, not new fractures.

> This is a substantial improvement, but I would not give the blanket "all as recommended" approval yet. The remaining problems are specific enough to fix in the briefs; they do not require another round of system design.

>
> The new structure produces actual drama. Lucien's investigation costs Sal work and Elisabet access. Seraphine pursues a statement the bereaved family wants withdrawn. Elisabet's return depends on conditions that later obstruct her research. These are choices that change other people's options, rather than disagreements resolved through better listening.
>
> The Companions principles are reaching the scene design: characters misread, make consequential choices, and sometimes respond constructively. The remaining danger is replacing universal consideration with universal withholding.
>
> I would correct these before deriving the affected prose packets:
>
> 1. The Medicaid correction still contains a live contradiction.
>     E03's notes retain: "If the author confirms … her own lapsed CPR certification, she confesses both here." A later note correctly rules that reading out. Delete the obsolete alternative from the active brief.
>     E01 also declares the coverage rules "reasonable in themselves." That judgment is unnecessary and reinstates the automatic institutional legitimacy you rejected. Describe the notice, address and unfinished renewal. Let readers judge.
> 2. The stakeout risks looking deliberately sabotaged by the author.
>     E39 prohibits a face, plate, logo, pursuit and usable identification; Seraphine's hand supplies the escape. That can work, but its physical circumstances must earn it. Why can't they record the car? What immediate danger does she perceive? Why does holding his arm prevent every other useful observation?
>     Keep the limited discovery, but give their failure a convincing practical cause. Otherwise readers will see the sequel being protected.
>     The packet also alternates between calling complaint-line access proof and calling it Lucien's inference. Choose the supported evidentiary status consistently.
> 3. The ending's lost observation needs a clear sequence.
>     E49 says Seraphine's water shivers, then she leaves for the stranger, yet her departure costs the record its onset. Establish whether she leaves before the onset, misses recording something she witnesses, or loses a different measurement. The sacrifice should be intelligible without consulting the control layer.
>     Likewise, a missing case establishes its removal; it does not by itself establish that someone successfully "measured their window."
> 4. B02's missing testimony has an obvious escape route.
>     E33 makes Seraphine leave a public meeting before speaking, so Rieux's log dominates the later review. Why can't she submit her account in writing, particularly given her newly central desire to get her account recorded?
>     This needs a concrete obstacle or a consequential choice not to submit. Overload during one meeting does not yet explain prolonged silence.
> 5. The consent conflict needs sharper terms.
>     Elisabet's renewed university sponsorship is a strong return condition. But the proposal moves from "public data and consented data" to calling Caro's EMS material the "only data" she can use. Those are different constraints.
>     Specify what Caro can authorize, what the original scope permits, and why the later finding exceeds it. Otherwise the permission boundary may appear whenever the plot needs an argument.
>

>

> **On trilogy-specific conflict expectations (the same message):**
>
> Yes to trilogy-specific expectations; I would avoid hard scene quotas. Your proposed movement is a strong default: each trilogy begins with people struggling to work together, then increasingly tests what they can accomplish together against outside opposition.
>
> The crucial distinction is less internal obstruction, not less internal difference. By a trilogy's end, characters can disagree fiercely and still act together. That is earned cohesion. Everyone becoming reasonable would return us to the original problem.
>
> I would track three things separately:
>
> * Personal pressure: desire, fear, divided loyalty, self-deception. This can remain high throughout.
> * Conflict among protagonists: incompatible aims or methods that change what someone does.
> * External opposition: another person or institution actively constraining, exploiting or defeating their efforts. The phenomenon itself also creates danger, but it should not be counted as antagonist action.
>
> Otherwise, a character's anxiety, an argument and a faction attack all get counted as equivalent "conflict," and the measurement tells us little.
>
> Your internal-to-external progression should recur with a different purpose in each trilogy:
>
> | Trilogy | Opening emphasis | Middle movement | Closing balance |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Veil, B01–03 | Forming relationships; incompatible interpretations and working methods. External actors are only partly understood. | Choices expose institutional interests and make cooperation expensive. | External consequences dominate. Some trust is earned; consequential disagreements remain unfinished through Baz's death. |
> | Neon, B04–06 | Grief, separation and competing commitments disrupt the group. These are consequences of Veil, not a reset to introductory mistrust. | Factions offer things characters actually want. Alliances become difficult to reverse. | External confrontation dominates, while one or two internal divisions reach their greatest cost around the Rupture and Tahl's death. |
> | Loom, B07–09 | Survivors disagree over what is still worth doing and what they are entitled to sacrifice. Established trust should still matter. | They build a coalition capable of action without settling every dispute. | External opposition peaks. Internal conflict concentrates on the terms and cost of the Mending; cooperation becomes a chosen act. |
>
> I would modify your proposed curve at the climax. Internal conflict should usually decline in frequency, while its remaining decisions increase in importance. Ten minor arguments become one consequential disagreement over the plan. That disagreement can be the climax's human substance.
>
> Similarly, external conflict should grow in reach, specificity and consequence, not simply appear more often. A faction making one offer that splits the group can do more than three attacks.
>
> For thresholds, I would use floors for dramatic function and ceilings for repetition, rather than percentages:
>
> | Measure | Useful floor | Useful ceiling |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Protagonist agency | Every act contains a protagonist's initiative that materially changes the situation. | No long sequence in which the leads only receive information and respond. |
> | Internal conflict | Each book contains a consequential disagreement that affects action or a relationship beyond its originating scene. | Don't make every relationship strained simultaneously or use withholding as everyone's response. |
> | External opposition | Opposition has an identifiable objective and makes consequential moves appropriate to that stage of the saga. Its identity may remain unknown to the characters. | Don't turn every ordinary inconvenience into faction activity or interrupt every quiet scene with menace. |
> | Earned cooperation | Each book lets readers experience the practical benefit or pleasure of people working well together. | Cooperation cannot erase previously established costs without cause. |
> | Escalation | By each trilogy's end, earlier choices have narrowed the available options. | Avoid escalating every dimension at once: danger, spectacle, betrayal, casualties and mystery. |
>
> These are review triggers, not instructions to manufacture a scene. A book may meet a function through a few strong sequences rather than frequent incidents.
>
> Across all nine books, I would protect two continuing movements:
>
> The group becomes better at cooperating. Later disagreements should increasingly concern substantive choices—whose safety, whose freedom, which sacrifice—rather than failures to communicate that they have already learned to overcome.
>
> Outside opposition becomes more entangled with their own aims. Veil asks what is happening and who is using it. Neon makes the competing promises attractive and costly. Loom forces a decision about what kind of world their intervention will create. That progression gives the factions dramatic purpose beyond supplying more resistance.
>
> For the current work, this means B01 needs enough external action to drive its mystery, even while internal conflict dominates its relationships. And B03 should not be saturated with fresh interpersonal fractures merely because the earlier draft was too harmonious. Readers need to experience what this group has built before they lose Baz.
>
> My recommendation is a compact saga-level guide with these trilogy trajectories, followed by an act-level review of the existing beats. Don't add numerical floors and ceilings yet. First see the actual distribution of initiative, obstruction, cooperation and consequence. Numbers can then reveal a pattern; they should not decide how often these people must fight.
