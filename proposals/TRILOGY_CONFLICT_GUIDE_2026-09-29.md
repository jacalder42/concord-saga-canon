# Trilogy conflict guide

Status: APPROVED DESIGN (Tier B), not ruled canon. Written 2026-09-29 under Q-TG1
(`decisions/REVISED_EBCI_VEIL_PASS6_AND_TRILOGY_GUIDE_AUTHOR_ANSWERS_2026-09-29.md`), from the author's idea of
trilogy-specific expectations as developed in the forwarded review
(`reports/REVISED_EBCI_AND_VEIL_PASS6_REVIEW_RECONCILIATION_2026-09-29.md` §4 and Appendix).

**What it does not change:** no ruling, milestone, card, beat or packet. **It sets no quotas and no numbers.** It is a
lens for reviewing architecture act by act. It is never an instruction to manufacture a scene, and never a drafting
line.

## 1. The shape

**Each trilogy moves from people struggling to work together toward testing what they can do together against
outside opposition.** The movement is **less internal obstruction, not less internal difference.** By a trilogy's end
the characters can disagree hard and still act together; that is earned cohesion. Everyone becoming reasonable would
bring back the original problem.

**At a climax:**
- Internal conflict falls in frequency and rises in importance: ten small arguments become one consequential
  disagreement about the plan, and that disagreement can be the climax's human substance.
- External conflict grows in reach, specificity and consequence, not in frequency. A faction making one offer that
  splits the group can do more than three attacks.

## 2. Three things, tracked separately

| Dimension | What counts | What does not count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Personal pressure** | Desire, fear, divided loyalty, self-deception. It can stay high throughout | Conflict between people |
| **Conflict among protagonists** | Incompatible aims or methods that **change what someone does** | A difference of view resolved inside the scene |
| **External opposition** | Another person or institution actively constraining, exploiting or defeating the protagonists' efforts, with an identifiable objective (their identity may stay unknown to the characters) | The phenomenon's danger. Ordinary inconvenience with no will behind it (Q-XP2: ordinary people keep their own motives) |

**Modes of conflict among protagonists.** Name the mode when reviewing, because one mode repeated everywhere becomes a
uniform emotional world:
- **withholding**;
- **open action against another's wishes**;
- **bargaining**;
- **competing**;
- **a truth told that forces an unwelcome decision**;
- **blame carried.**

**No automatic repair is not the same as honesty never helping.** Sometimes a truth does help.

## 3. The trilogies

| Trilogy | Opening | Middle | Close |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Veil, B01–B03** | Relationships forming; incompatible interpretations and working methods. External actors are only partly understood (B01: unattributed hands that want only the time) | Choices expose institutional interests and make cooperation expensive (B02: the order, the price, the method in Dominion hands) | External consequences dominate. Some trust is earned, and consequential disagreements stay unfinished through Baz's death. **B03 is not saturated with fresh fractures: readers need to experience what this group has built before they lose Baz** |
| **Neon, B04–B06** | Grief, separation and competing commitments disrupt the group. **These are consequences of Veil, not a reset to introductory mistrust** | Factions offer things the characters actually want. Alliances become hard to reverse | External confrontation dominates. One or two internal divisions reach their greatest cost around the Rupture and Tahl's death |
| **Loom, B07–B09** | Survivors disagree over what is still worth doing and what they are entitled to sacrifice. Established trust still matters | They build a coalition that can act without settling every dispute | External opposition peaks. Internal conflict concentrates on the terms and cost of the Mending; cooperation becomes a chosen act |

**Across the saga, two movements are protected:**
1. **The group gets better at cooperating.** Later disagreements concern substantive choices (whose safety, whose
   freedom, which sacrifice), not failures to communicate that they have already learned to overcome.
2. **The opposition becomes entangled with their own aims.** Veil asks what is happening and who is using it. Neon
   makes the competing promises attractive and costly. Loom forces a decision about what kind of world their
   intervention will make.

## 4. Review triggers

**These are floors for dramatic function and ceilings for repetition. They are not percentages.** Where one fires, it
prompts a look at the act; it is not an instruction to add a scene. A book may meet a function through a few strong
sequences.

| Measure | Floor | Ceiling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Protagonist agency** | Every act holds a protagonist's initiative that materially changes the situation | No long run in which the leads only receive information and respond |
| **Conflict among protagonists** | Each book holds a consequential disagreement that affects action or a relationship beyond its scene | Not every relationship strained at once; not withholding as everyone's response |
| **External opposition** | The opposition has an identifiable objective and makes consequential moves fit for its stage of the saga | Not every ordinary inconvenience turned into faction activity; not every quiet scene interrupted by menace |
| **Earned cooperation** | Each book lets readers feel the practical benefit or pleasure of people working well together | Cooperation never erases established costs without a cause |
| **Escalation** | By a trilogy's end, earlier choices have narrowed the options | Not every dimension escalated at once: danger, spectacle, betrayal, casualties and mystery |

**No numbers yet.** First look at the actual distribution of initiative, obstruction, cooperation and consequence.
Numbers may later reveal a pattern; they do not decide how often these people fight.

## 5. The test

Whether the characters' actions make the book compelling to read, not whether every box says yes.
