# Concord Saga Narrative Audit Framework v1

Status: PROPOSED / NON-CANONICAL DIAGNOSTIC
Purpose: Peer-calibrated structural review of book and episode architecture before deep prose expansion.
Scope: All nine Concord Saga novels; first application is Veil Book 1.
Authority: Diagnostic only. This file does not alter canon, beats, rulings, or prose.

## 1. Why this audit exists

The audit tests whether a planned novel offers a varied, inhabitable reader experience rather than merely advancing plot. It is derived from comparative study of successful fantasy, urban fantasy, progression/LitRPG, YA fantasy, and romantasy series, including Dungeon Crawler Carl, He Who Fights With Monsters, Rivers of London, Harry Potter, A Court of Thorns and Roses, Fourth Wing/Empyrean, Shades of Magic, Iron Druid, Primal Hunter, and Bride.

The framework does not prescribe imitation, fixed word counts, or fixed numbers of fights. It asks whether each book creates:
- frequent reader rewards,
- clear emotional focus,
- accumulated place-memory,
- varied narrative modes,
- anticipated events,
- sufficient pleasure and ordinary life,
- escalating but non-monotonic pressure,
- meaningful aftermath,
- callbacks and convergence rather than endless introduction of new material.

## 2. Core unit

The audit unit is the planned narrative episode, not necessarily the final published chapter.

A future print/digital chapter may contain one to several episodes.

Working envelope for a typical Concord novel:
- roughly 55-80 narrative episodes,
- roughly 25-45 eventual conventional chapters,
- 6-10 major tentpoles,
- 15-25 medium narrative events,
- at least one meaningful reader reward in most episodes.

These are diagnostic ranges, not hard rules.

## 3. Episode audit fields

For every episode record:

1. Episode ID / title
2. Approximate movement / act
3. Dominant Narrative Mode
4. Primary Reader Reward
5. Secondary Reader Reward(s)
6. Active emotional-core characters
7. Active ensemble/supporting characters
8. Location
9. Location tier: Home / Recurring / Set Piece / Texture
10. Anticipated event seeded, advanced, or paid off
11. New concept introduced
12. Existing concept deepened
13. Callback / breadcrumb / payoff
14. Relationship movement
15. Danger / action level
16. Wonder level
17. Humor / pleasure element
18. Recovery / aftermath function
19. State change: what is different at episode end?
20. Audit notes / flags

## 4. Narrative Mode codes

Each episode receives one dominant mode:

- LIVE — ordinary existence, work, food, music, travel, domestic or social life
- SEEK — investigation, research, pursuit, discovery
- CONNECT — relationship, friendship, social interaction, intimacy
- WONDER — magical/supernatural encounter, awe, strangeness
- PRESSURE — threat, tension, dread, deadline, pursuit
- ACTION — sustained physical conflict, escape, chase, combat
- RECOVER — aftermath, grief, healing, processing, regrouping
- CONVERGE — previously separate threads materially collide

Secondary modes may be noted, but the dominant mode is used to visualize rhythm.

## 5. Reader Reward codes

Every episode should normally give the reader at least one meaningful reward.

- CHAR — character insight or development
- REL — relationship movement
- WON — wonder / awe / supernatural strangeness
- FUN — humor, pleasure, competence, food, music, social enjoyment
- MYS — mystery movement or question sharpening
- DISC — discovery / revelation
- ACT — action / danger payoff
- HEAT — attraction, romance, intimacy, sex
- WORLD — meaningful world texture
- PLOT — consequential external development
- PAY — payoff or satisfying callback to earlier setup

No single reward type is privileged. Repetition and long reward droughts are diagnostic concerns.

## 6. Character bandwidth tiers

Track simultaneous cognitive/emotional load rather than raw named-character count.

- Tier A — Emotional Core: approximately 3-6 simultaneously important characters
- Tier B — Active Ensemble: approximately 6-12 recurring characters
- Tier C — World Cast: approximately 10-25+ recurring characters across the book
- Tier D — Local Cast: temporary act/book/location characters; no fixed cap

Flags:
- too many Tier A characters at once,
- newly introduced characters competing with unresolved established arcs,
- supporting characters used only as plot utilities,
- long absences that erase intended emotional importance,
- multiple characters serving indistinguishable narrative functions.

## 7. Location bandwidth tiers

- Tier A / Home: approximately 1-3 dominant emotional anchors per book
- Tier B / Recurring World: approximately 4-10 significant repeated locations
- Tier C / Set Piece: approximately 3-8 purpose-built event locations
- Tier D / Texture: incidental streets, restaurants, hotels, transit, shops, etc.

Primary audit question:
Does revisiting a location accumulate history and emotional meaning, or merely reuse a backdrop?

## 8. Anticipation track

Explicitly record future events the reader can look forward to or dread:
- performance,
- party,
- dinner,
- date,
- expedition,
- ritual,
- meeting,
- festival,
- trip,
- arrival/departure,
- confrontation,
- funeral,
- wedding,
- scheduled Resonance event,
- other promised set piece.

Anticipated events provide propulsion without requiring constant danger.

## 9. Newness vs deepening

For each episode distinguish:
- NEW: a genuinely new person, place, rule, faction, magical concept, or major question,
- DEEPEN: new information or emotional meaning attached to something already established,
- PAY: a prior element returns and matters.

Desired saga trend:
- Veil may introduce relatively more,
- Neon should increasingly recombine,
- Loom should rely heavily on deepening, callbacks, returns, and convergence.

Flag long sequences dominated by NEW with little DEEPEN/PAY.

## 10. Pleasure track

The audit must explicitly look for non-threat reader pleasure:
- music,
- food,
- beauty,
- humor,
- competence,
- friendship,
- attraction,
- sex/intimacy,
- local culture,
- playful magic,
- successful small goals,
- private jokes,
- ordinary routines the reader learns to value.

Core principle:
Readers need reasons to enjoy inhabiting the world before later books can make the loss or corruption of that world expensive.

## 11. Set-piece definition

A set piece is not synonymous with combat.

A set piece is a sustained sequence with distinctive location or circumstance, heightened anticipation/stakes/emotion, and a consequence that persists.

Types:
- Action
- Wonder
- Social
- Romance / Heat
- Mystery / Discovery
- Ceremony / Ritual
- Grief / Aftermath

Audit both combat and noncombat set-piece cadence.

## 12. Movement model for an approximately 70-episode novel

This is a diagnostic lens, not a mandatory seven-act structure.

### Movement I — Invitation (approx. EP01-10)
Introduce desired life and the wrongness entering it.
Reader should encounter appealing cast/place elements quickly.

### Movement II — Seduction (approx. EP11-20)
Make the world increasingly worth inhabiting while danger or mystery grows.
Build routines, recurring places, attractions, jokes, and ensemble familiarity.

### Movement III — Investment (approx. EP21-30)
Give the reader people, places, and relationships worth losing.
Pay at least one anticipated event; deepen a major mystery.

### Movement IV — Expansion (approx. EP31-40)
Reveal that the story is larger than initially understood.
Prefer meaningful implication over mandatory explosive midpoint action.

### Movement V — Complication (approx. EP41-50)
Previously separate systems begin interacting.
Relationships create plot consequences; world/magic discoveries affect trust and choices.

### Movement VI — Acceleration (approx. EP51-60)
Reduce pure introductions. Increase callbacks, pressures, legibility of breadcrumbs, and consequence.

### Movement VII — Convergence (approx. EP61-70)
Preparation, fear, arrival, reversal, action/discovery, choice, consequence, and aftermath.
Do not devote the entire final movement to uninterrupted combat.

## 13. Sequence-level diagnostic flags

Flag:
- 4+ consecutive episodes with the same dominant mode unless deliberately justified,
- repeated action/pressure without recovery or contrast,
- repeated investigation/exposition without interpersonal or experiential reward,
- multiple episodes with no identifiable state change,
- reader-reward droughts,
- overuse of PLOT as primary reward,
- major set pieces with insufficient anticipation,
- major consequences with insufficient aftermath,
- home locations that fail to accumulate emotional history,
- too many new concepts in a short span,
- recurring characters disappearing without intentional effect,
- romance isolated from plot/world rather than interacting with them,
- wonder appearing only as threat or exposition,
- humor/pleasure disappearing for long stretches without intentional tonal reason.

## 14. Book-level output: Narrative ECG

For every audited book produce:

A. Episode table using the fields in Section 3.
B. Narrative-mode sequence.
C. Reader-reward distribution.
D. Character-bandwidth curve.
E. Location-use map.
F. New vs Deepen vs Pay curve.
G. Anticipated-event timeline.
H. Action / danger cadence.
I. Wonder cadence.
J. Relationship / heat cadence.
K. Pleasure / humor / ordinary-life cadence.
L. Recovery / aftermath cadence.
M. Set-piece map.
N. Diagnostic flags.
O. Strengths worth protecting.
P. Suggested revisions ranked by structural leverage.

The audit should diagnose before rewriting.

## 15. Veil Book 1 first-pass questions

1. Does New Orleans become home, or merely scenery?
2. Does the reader get enough ordinary Seraphine/Caro/Lucien/ensemble life to care about later disruption?
3. Are major magical concepts introduced faster than they are emotionally metabolized?
4. Does each episode offer a clear reader reward?
5. Are action and pressure balanced by pleasure, wonder, connection, and recovery?
6. Are romantic/relationship beats integrated with discovery and consequence?
7. Are supporting characters recurring with purpose rather than appearing as utilities?
8. Are there enough anticipated events to create propulsion without threat?
9. Does the midpoint expand the perceived story rather than simply intensify danger?
10. Does the ending include consequence and aftermath, not only climax?
11. Are breadcrumbs and callbacks sufficiently spaced to reward attentive reading?
12. Does Book 1 leave the reader wanting more time with these people, not merely answers to lore questions?

## 16. Use across the saga

Apply the same fields to all nine books, but do not demand identical distributions.

Expected broad progression:
- VEIL: discovery, intimacy, mystery, wonder, local life, accumulating attachment.
- NEON: faction pressure, widening geography, consequence, action, ideological collision.
- LOOM: recombination, return, callback, payoff, loss, repair, convergence.

The framework should reveal difference between books, not flatten them into a single formula.

## 17. Governance

- Audit outputs are diagnostic.
- No audit finding automatically changes canon or approved beats.
- Proposed beat changes should remain proposals until author review.
- Preserve original beat IDs and traceability.
- When evidence is ambiguous, record uncertainty rather than inventing missing material.
- Peer-series comparisons are calibration references, not authorities over Concord's voice or design.
